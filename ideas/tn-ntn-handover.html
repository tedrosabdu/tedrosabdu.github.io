Rethinking Handover for Integrated TN–NTN Networks

TN/NTN
Mobility
Handover
6G
AI/ML
Research perspectives on intelligent mobility and handover across integrated terrestrial and non-terrestrial networks.
Published

September 21, 2026

The integration of terrestrial networks (TN) and non-terrestrial networks (NTN) creates new opportunities for ubiquitous and resilient connectivity. It also changes the nature of mobility management.

In a conventional terrestrial network, handover is primarily associated with user movement between cells. In NTN, the network itself may be moving: satellites and their coverage areas evolve continuously relative to users on the ground.

When TN and NTN are integrated, mobility management therefore becomes a broader decision problem involving radio conditions, satellite visibility, user demand, network availability, service requirements, and handover cost.

My research in this area has evolved from demand-aware handover in LEO constellations toward intelligent mobility management for integrated TN–NTN systems.

From LEO Handover to TN–NTN Mobility

LEO constellations introduce an inherent mobility challenge. Even for a stationary user, the serving satellite remains visible only for a limited period.

This leads to a fundamental question:

How can continuous service be maintained while avoiding unnecessary handovers?

In our earlier work on demand-aware flexible handover for LEO constellations, we considered handover together with user traffic demand. The objective was to reduce the number of handovers while continuously satisfying the required user demand.

This work motivated a broader perspective: handover should not depend only on which satellite is visible or which link is strongest. The decision should also consider whether the serving network can continue satisfying the user’s service requirements.

The problem becomes more complex in integrated TN–NTN systems because a user may have multiple connectivity alternatives, including terrestrial cells, satellite beams, and different satellites.

The decision therefore changes from:

Which satellite should serve the user?

to:

Which network, cell, beam, or satellite should serve the user, and when is switching actually beneficial?

Handover Is More Than a Radio Decision

Integrated TN–NTN mobility involves several interacting factors:

  • radio-link quality,
  • satellite visibility and coverage evolution,
  • terrestrial-network availability,
  • user mobility,
  • traffic and service requirements,
  • network load,
  • handover interruption,
  • connection failures,
  • and ping-pong behavior.

There is also an architectural dimension.

Terrestrial connectivity may be provided by a Mobile Network Operator (MNO) while satellite connectivity is provided by a Satellite Network Operator (SNO).

Our work on MNO–SNO cooperation examined how roaming and network cooperation affect mobility between terrestrial and satellite networks. This highlights an important point: seamless TN–NTN mobility depends not only on the handover algorithm but also on the architecture supporting the transition.

Future handover mechanisms may therefore need to reason jointly about radio conditions and network context.

From Rule-Based to Intelligent Handover

Conventional handover mechanisms commonly use radio measurements together with parameters such as thresholds, hysteresis, and time-to-trigger.

These mechanisms remain important, but integrated TN–NTN environments introduce additional information that could potentially improve mobility decisions.

For example, a handover controller could consider:

Radio context — signal quality, interference, and link availability.

NTN context — satellite position, elevation, visibility duration, and beam evolution.

User context — mobility, traffic demand, and service requirements.

Network context — load, resource availability, and candidate-network conditions.

Architectural context — MNO/SNO cooperation and expected handover interruption.

Predictive context — future satellite visibility and expected network conditions.

This changes handover from a largely reactive event toward a potentially context-aware and predictive decision process.

Learning-Based Mobility Management

Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) provides one framework for studying such sequential mobility decisions.

Instead of optimizing each handover independently, an RL agent can learn a policy that considers the longer-term consequences of remaining with the current network or moving to another candidate.

In our recent work, we evaluated PPO, A2C, and DQN for handover management across hybrid TN–NTN scenarios.

An important observation is that the relative behavior of these algorithms depends on the considered coverage environment. This suggests that simply introducing AI/ML does not automatically solve the mobility problem.

The state representation, reward formulation, network scenario, training environment, and algorithm all matter.

For me, this leads to a more interesting research question:

How can mobility intelligence adapt to the characteristics of the TN–NTN environment rather than relying on one fixed handover strategy?

Research Directions

Building on these results, I am particularly interested in four directions.

1. Context-Aware and Predictive Handover

Satellite trajectories provide information that is fundamentally different from conventional terrestrial mobility: much of the satellite motion is predictable.

This creates opportunities to combine current radio measurements with information about:

  • future satellite visibility,
  • expected coverage evolution,
  • user trajectory,
  • service requirements,
  • and network conditions.

A key question is whether this information can enable proactive handover decisions rather than waiting until link degradation triggers a transition.

2. Hybrid Optimization and Learning

My earlier LEO handover work used optimization-based methods, while more recent work investigates learning-based mobility management.

These approaches have complementary strengths.

Model-based optimization can exploit known system structure and constraints. Learning can be useful when decisions involve complex dynamics and long-term consequences.

This motivates hybrid approaches such as:

  • optimization-guided reinforcement learning,
  • learning-assisted candidate selection,
  • model-based constraints for learned policies,
  • and hierarchical TN–NTN decision making.

The objective is not simply to replace optimization with AI, but to determine where learning adds measurable value.

3. Service- and Resource-Aware Mobility

The strongest instantaneous radio link may not always be the most appropriate serving network.

Future mobility decisions could jointly consider:

  • link quality,
  • user QoS,
  • traffic demand,
  • terrestrial and satellite resources,
  • network congestion,
  • and expected connection duration.

This connects mobility management with another major area of my research: radio-resource management.

A broader question is therefore:

Can mobility and resource management be optimized jointly rather than treated as independent network functions?

4. Mobility-Aware TN–NTN Architecture

The network architecture itself can influence handover latency, signaling, and service continuity.

This makes MNO–SNO cooperation, core-network integration, and mobility procedures part of the research problem.

An important direction is to investigate which architectural assumptions enable intelligent mobility mechanisms to operate effectively and how mobility algorithms should adapt when those assumptions change.

Research Perspective

The evolution from LEO satellite handover toward integrated TN–NTN mobility suggests that future handover mechanisms will need to operate across several dimensions:

radio conditions + satellite dynamics + user requirements + network resources + architecture + prediction

The research challenge is therefore broader than finding a better handover threshold.

The objective is to determine when to hand over, where to hand over, and whether a handover is worthwhile, while maintaining service continuity and using terrestrial and non-terrestrial resources efficiently.

This is the direction I am continuing to explore through system modeling, mathematical optimization, machine learning, and system-level evaluation.
