From Satellite Resource Planning to Constellation Optimization

Satellite Networks
Constellation Design
Resource Planning
Optimization
6G
Direct-to-Device
Research perspectives on connecting service requirements, satellite resource planning, and constellation design for future 6G satellite networks.
Published

September 21, 2026

Designing a satellite network involves decisions at multiple levels.

At the satellite level, we need to determine what communication and payload capabilities are required. At the network level, we need to determine how many satellites are needed, how they should be deployed, and whether the resulting constellation can satisfy the intended service requirements.

My recent research approaches these questions from two connected directions: satellite resource planning and constellation optimization.

Together, they motivate a broader question:

How can communication-service requirements be translated into satellite capabilities and constellation architectures that are technically feasible and resource-efficient?

From Resource Allocation to Resource Planning

Much of satellite resource management starts with an existing system and asks how available resources should be allocated.

Typical decisions involve:

  • power,
  • bandwidth,
  • beams,
  • payload resources,
  • and traffic demand.

Resource planning asks an earlier question:

What resources should the satellite system have before it is deployed?

This distinction is important because service requirements influence not only how resources are allocated, but potentially the physical and system-level characteristics of the satellite.

In our work on resource planning for next-generation satellite communication systems, we investigated how communication requirements can be translated into candidate satellite-system specifications.

The planning framework considers parameters associated with:

  • spot beams,
  • DC power,
  • antenna dimensions,
  • satellite mass,
  • and system cost.

This creates a connection between communication-system requirements and satellite-system design.

From Satellite Planning to Constellation Design

For a non-geostationary satellite network, designing an individual satellite is only part of the problem.

Network performance also depends on the constellation architecture, including:

  • orbital altitude,
  • number of satellites,
  • orbital geometry,
  • coverage,
  • satellite visibility,
  • and communication-link requirements.

This leads to a second question:

Given the capabilities of the satellites, what constellation is required to deliver the target service?

Our recent work on satellite constellation optimization for 6G and beyond investigates this problem in the context of connectivity to handheld devices.

The formulation considers communication and visibility requirements together with orbital characteristics and seeks to determine a constellation satisfying the considered requirements with a reduced number of satellites.

This connects orbital design with communication-system performance.

Coverage Is Not the Same as Service

An important distinction in satellite-network planning is between geometric coverage and service availability.

A satellite may be visible to a user without necessarily providing a communication link capable of satisfying the target service.

This distinction becomes particularly important for Direct-to-Device (D2D) connectivity, where handheld-terminal capabilities impose challenging link-budget constraints.

For future constellation design, the relevant question is therefore not simply:

Is a satellite visible?

but:

Can a visible satellite provide the required service?

This motivates a more service-aware approach to constellation planning.

The Constellation Design Trade-Off

Orbital altitude illustrates the interaction between constellation architecture and communication performance.

Lower-altitude satellites benefit from shorter communication distances, but an individual satellite covers a smaller geographic region and a larger constellation may therefore be required.

At higher altitudes, individual satellites can cover larger regions, but the longer communication distance makes the link budget more demanding.

This creates a coupled design problem involving:

altitude ↔︎ constellation size ↔︎ satellite capability ↔︎ communication performance

Studying these quantities jointly is more informative than optimizing any one of them in isolation.

Research Directions

Building on the two studies, I am particularly interested in four broader directions.

1. Joint Resource and Constellation Optimization

Satellite resource planning and constellation optimization are naturally connected.

Instead of designing satellite capabilities first and determining constellation size afterward, a future framework could optimize them jointly.

For example, there may be a trade-off between deploying:

  • more satellites with relatively modest capabilities, or
  • fewer satellites with greater communication and payload capabilities.

A joint formulation could expose trade-offs involving satellite count, mass, power, antenna characteristics, capacity, and cost.

The central question is:

What combination of satellite capability and constellation architecture provides the required service most efficiently?

2. Traffic- and Service-Aware Constellation Planning

Global communication demand is highly non-uniform.

Population distribution, transportation corridors, maritime and aviation traffic, and terrestrial-network availability all affect where satellite capacity is actually required.

This suggests moving beyond uniform geographic coverage toward traffic-aware and service-aware planning.

A possible design chain is:

traffic demand → service requirements → satellite resources → constellation architecture

This could also incorporate different requirements for broadband, IoT, emergency connectivity, or Direct-to-Device services.

3. Mobility-Aware TN–NTN Constellation Design

Constellation architecture determines more than coverage.

Satellite density, altitude, coverage overlap, and visibility duration also influence:

  • handover frequency,
  • candidate-satellite availability,
  • connection duration,
  • and mobility robustness.

This creates a direct connection with my research on TN–NTN mobility and handover.

A future constellation could therefore be designed not only for coverage and capacity, but also for efficient mobility.

In an integrated TN–NTN system, terrestrial coverage could also become part of the planning problem:

Where and when does NTN actually need to provide coverage or additional capacity given the capabilities of the terrestrial network?

This connects satellite planning with the broader architecture of future integrated networks.

4. Adaptive and Learning-Assisted Planning

Satellite systems operate for many years, while traffic demand and service requirements can evolve significantly.

Planning solely for one expected operating condition may therefore result in an architecture that becomes inefficient as demand changes.

Future approaches could incorporate:

  • uncertainty in traffic demand,
  • flexible or software-defined payload capabilities,
  • robust and scenario-based optimization,
  • multi-objective optimization,
  • and learning-assisted search.

AI/ML may be particularly useful for accelerating large design-space exploration or learning surrogate models for expensive system evaluations.

However, I see learning as a complement to, rather than an automatic replacement for, mathematical and model-based optimization.

The key question is:

Where can learning provide a measurable advantage while preserving the physical structure and constraints of the satellite-system problem?

A Broader Research Framework

These directions suggest a broader view of satellite-network design:

Service Requirements
↓
Traffic & Coverage Modeling
↓
Satellite Resource Planning
↓
Constellation Optimization
↓
Dynamic Resource & Mobility Management

The interesting research opportunity is that these stages do not necessarily need to be independent.

Mobility requirements could influence constellation design. Terrestrial coverage could influence required satellite capacity. Payload flexibility could influence how aggressively a system must be optimized before deployment.

This creates the possibility of moving from individual optimization problems toward system-level co-design.

Research Perspective

My earlier work focused primarily on managing resources within satellite systems. These more recent studies move the question progressively toward planning what resources should exist and what network architecture should provide them.

The broader question therefore becomes:

What satellite resources should be deployed, what constellation architecture should provide them, and how should those resources subsequently be managed?

I see three connected levels:

Design — what system should be built?

Planning — what resources and architecture are required to support the intended services?

Operation — how should those resources be dynamically managed after deployment?

Connecting these levels is an interesting direction toward satellite networks that are more service-aware, resource-efficient, flexible, and responsive to changing demand.
